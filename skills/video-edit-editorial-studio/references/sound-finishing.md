# Sound and finishing

## Sound follows the sentence

Narration is the foreground. A subtle recorded riser builds expectation beneath the hook, then ends at the rhetorical turn. The reference began the riser 0.08s in, ended at 2.73s and used a 75ms release, with a restrained −25dBFS stem peak. Those values describe one source and mix; choose the new cue's level by its spectrum, speech masking and listening rather than copying an absolute gain.

The riser was derived from a licensed recorded whoosh using pitch-preserving tempo processing, band limiting and a gain envelope. No generated sound or music bed was required. Use existing recorded assets or silence when generation is prohibited. Music remains optional.

Smaller accents mark purposeful arrivals, file handling, typing and confirmations. A travel cue can lead the landing, while a click peak aligns to the visible resolution. Trim a cue by audible activity, not by its file start; use short fades to avoid clicks. Avoid one sound per glyph. Recheck timing after the final motion revision, especially when an older effect mix is inherited.

A percentage label in a colour demonstration may express effect strength. Infographic bars must not acquire invented measurement claims merely because sound gives them emphasis.

## Preserve and export

Use a frame clock and keep narration/video synchronization through the moving presenter window. A 60fps graphic composition can overlay 30fps camera footage; it does not create new captured camera frames. Preserve timing and pitch unless retiming is authorized.

Prepare rotation and HDR/SDR conversion before compositing. Render native text/vector geometry or sufficiently detailed raster layers. A practical reference export was H.264, 1080×1920, 60fps, BT.709 SDR, PNG capture and CRF16. These are measured reference settings, not an imposed delivery specification.

Set colour conversion and signalling explicitly; tagging an unconverted HDR image as SDR is not a grade. Avoid repeated lossy encodes. When picture changes and audio does not, stream-copy the full original audio track into the final file and compare its complete payload. Supply full sidecar captions even where expressive motion type replaces on-picture captions.

Keep source media, font binaries, sound files, reference clips and their rights in the private project. Public skills contain original guidance and redistributable helpers only. Saving or accepting a style does not authorize a push, publication, media upload or asset redistribution.

## Review the actual encoded result

Inspect the opening close frame, the busiest hook, each completed text phrase, every type slot, corner-window moves, grade restoration, media clearance and white-stage exits. Use consecutive frames around suspicious boundaries and normal-speed playback when available. Inspect native glyph edges as well as phone readability. A transition sample can legitimately show a partly revealed glyph; a held frame cannot.

For web-based rendering, complete decode alone does not detect uniform blank frames, tiled capture defects or stale layers. Inspect handoffs and, when a capture problem is suspected or previously observed, scan the entire decoded sequence for uniform frames, tiling and isolated outliers. Explain intentional blank white intervals separately.

Verify duration, expected frame count, dimensions, frame rate, stream starts, explicit colour, fast-start where needed and full A/V decode. Measure encoded loudness/true peak and audition when possible. For an unchanged audio payload, reuse its earlier measurements with that boundary stated. Do not infer timbre or subjective balance from a loudness number.

Report the exact export and editable source, what was visually inspected, what was measured, whether direct playback/listening occurred, and any remaining acceptance question. Technical checks, user creative approval, local skill installation and public release are distinct states.
