## Attention and editorial rhythm

### A. Alternate presence and explanation

**Use when:** A speaker establishes credibility but a mechanism is easier to show. R04 alternates head-and-shoulders teaching with full-screen process graphics; R06 gives complicated systems a dedicated visual stage.

**Construction:** Assemble the meaningful speech first. Mark places where the viewer needs proof, a relationship, or a demonstration. Carry the voice across a cut to the visual. Introduce its subject, perform the explanatory action, hold the result, then return to the speaker on interpretation or a new chapter. Where sound is continuous, avoid a distracting change in room tone at the picture cut.

**Adapt:** A film without a presenter can alternate a wide establishing shot with macro detail. A screen recording can switch between the actual interface and a simplified explanatory diagram. Trust and comprehension are the functions; talking heads and green boards are only one implementation.

**Checks:** Can the edit be understood with graphics hidden? Can the graphic be understood with captions hidden? If both are confusing, fix the story before adding another effect. Avoid returning to the presenter before an important diagram resolves.

### B. Use density changes as punctuation

**Use when:** A fast, dense sequence needs a reset. R01 clears its collage around 17–21s before presenting the raw-file metaphor; R05 returns from a network at roughly 16.5s to the speaker.

**Construction:** Define the density curve of the argument. Build information where relationships matter, clear irrelevant objects at a completed thought, and preserve one useful anchor. A clean hold can make the next reveal feel larger without a camera zoom.

**Adapt:** Replace a crowded graphic with one empty architectural shot, a quiet product detail, or a single word. Density is perceptual: six tiny labels may be less legible than one moving object.

**Checks:** Do not equate retention with constant motion. If a paused viewer cannot locate the important information, simplify.

## Type roles and caption rhythm

### C. A typographic role system

**Use when:** Titles, explanations, labels, and speech must coexist. Across the studies, an editorial serif announces the hook, red italic words carry emphasis, handwritten marks interpret, monospaced lines signify records, and bold sans captions support speech.

**Construction:** Assign roles before font selection. Set a hierarchy by size, contrast, position, and timing. Limit simultaneous type roles within a single frame. Place emphasis in a separate layer so it can be timed without shifting the entire title. Use real fonts and verify licence coverage; none of the source fonts are identified conclusively.

**Adapt:** A precise instrument film might use only a restrained sans and tabular numerals. A historical documentary might use a serif title and sober captions. A playful collage may tolerate more contrast. Do not transplant all five roles into every project.

**Checks:** Verify long names, punctuation, italics, ascenders/descenders, and small-screen rendering. Distinguish a caption from an annotation without relying only on colour. Never shrink critical copy to accommodate a decorative headline.

### D. Phrase captions with selective emphasis

**Use when:** Speech is quick and the viewer may watch silently. R01 and R02 use short accumulating fragments with selected yellow or red emphasis; later studies often rely on mostly white captions.

**Construction:** Correct the transcript. Build phrase units around actual speech and syntax, then time their reveal from word onsets. Reserve a stable caption area. Add words without repeatedly recentering each existing word, unless shifting is intentional. Highlight the concept that carries meaning, not every stressed syllable. Use subtle shadow or backing where footage lacks contrast.

**Adapt:** Choose full-phrase holds for calmer films; word highlighting for instruction; sparse labels for a music-led piece; a separate subtitle track when required. Silent films do not need invented narration or speech captions.

**Checks:** Avoid orphaned words, flashing one-frame captions, mistimed numbers, and adjacent captions whose changing widths make the baseline seem to jump. Read the exported version on a phone. Graphics must not hide the mouth or an important action. ASR is a draft, especially for names and technical terms.

## Staged evidence and annotation

### E. Source → selection → interpretation

**Use when:** The viewer needs evidence, not a decorative screenshot. R02 at 15.8–20.5s stages an announcement and marks the relevant portion; R03 at 16.8–21.5s isolates a quotation and progressively marks the terms being discussed.

**Construction:** Introduce one dominant source. Establish what it is, then crop or isolate the relevant passage. Reveal a circle, underline, highlight, bracket, or arrow only when its target becomes meaningful. Keep the underlying source visible enough to preserve context. Separate quotation from commentary.

**Implementation:** Parent the content, paper edge, tape, and shadow to one container. Put the highlight behind the text and above the paper. Reveal a line by animating a vector stroke length or a narrow matte along its path. Use an alpha matte or rectangular crop for a text excerpt. Apply the same transform to annotations and source when they move together.

**Adapt:** A documentary can use a clean document crop; a scientific explainer can bracket a chart region; a product film can isolate a functional detail. The material need not be paper. Replace screenshot piles with a single enlarged, readable proof where appropriate.

**Checks:** Preserve attribution and context, verify the claim independently when the task requires it, and do not fabricate endorsement. Decorative tiny screenshot text is not readable evidence. Highlighting a quote does not validate its factual content.

### F. Evidence accumulation without visual overload

**Use when:** Several examples establish breadth. R01 fans examples after one main source; R03 contrasts similar layouts; R05 uses many provider names as a category before tracing just two.

**Construction:** Set one primary plane or position. Introduce secondary items with different offsets and a small timing stagger. Reduce their size or contrast only after the viewer has understood their role. Finish the group, hold, then clear or select. Keep the main caption lane free.

**Adapt:** Use a measured grid, vertical index, split-screen comparison, or a sequence of full-screen shots instead of an overlapping pile. A comparison needs equal treatment; a main source with supporting examples needs unequal treatment.

**Checks:** Distinguish deliberate hierarchy from accidental overlap. More copies do not equal more proof. Do not distribute private screenshots or third-party media without rights.

## Physical objects and collage

### G. Paper arrival, attachment, and removal

**Use when:** A tactile, human, notebook-like direction suits the brief. R01 uses a ragged receipt; R04 uses paper strips and notes; R06 uses large taped pages.

**Construction:** Use a paper shape or licensed texture, irregular edge where appropriate, subtle off-white variation, a small coherent shadow, and an attachment mark. Group these with the content. Animate position and rotation about a chosen anchor. Settle once, then remain still while the viewer reads. For removal, peel, lift, slide, or cut on meaning; do not force a physical simulation.

**Starting point:** Arrive over 0.25–0.45s, rotate by a few degrees, and settle with a small correction. This is a proposed recipe. Use longer timing for weight, shorter for a playful sticker. Paper should not wobble indefinitely.

**Adapt:** Make the same container a transparent technical plate, a photographed object, a typographic panel, or a clean label. Tactility is optional.

**Checks:** A shadow should agree with the imagined light and depth. Texture must survive compression without degrading text. Keep torn edges away from information. A downloaded source's soft edge may be compression, not intended feathering.

### H. Receipt or log that develops a claim

**Use when:** Quantity, sequence, provenance, or a change in state is important. R01 builds an edit receipt, R02 accumulates repeated paid requests, and R06 isolates a failed item in a final log.

**Construction:** Set aligned columns and consistent line spacing. Reveal rows in meaningful groups. Extend the backing with a mask or scale an edge-anchored container rather than stretching the text. After the list becomes understandable, reveal a summary or highlight one decisive row. Exit as a grouped object.

**Adapt:** A medical explainer might use a timeline; a sport edit might use a scoreboard; an engineering demo might use a compact console; a product film might use calibrated measurement marks. Show real values or clearly illustrative data.

**Checks:** Do not fabricate performance statistics, proof, or financial totals. Dense rows can communicate volume but must not be presented as legible detail. If one row matters, enlarge it or isolate it. Match the highlight to the narration, not a fixed delay.

## Mechanism, contrast, and state

### I. Known state beside unfamiliar state

**Use when:** A new concept can be anchored in something familiar. R02 keeps the earlier numbered note visible while adding its counterpart around 7.5s.

**Construction:** Give both states comparable visual weight. Establish the familiar state, add the new state alongside it, then mark the important difference. Maintain the first state long enough for comparison. Replace or remove it when the argument moves on.

**Adapt:** Use before/after footage, aligned product details, two timelines, colour channels, or a spatial diagram. If chronology is the message, a change within one object may be clearer than side-by-side panels.

**Checks:** A comparison should control irrelevant differences such as scale, lighting, crop, and duration. Do not imply a fair comparison with unequal evidence.

### J. Explain a mechanism through state changes

**Use when:** A process should be understood causally. R04 moves highlighted code values into a separate destination diagram; R06 builds and then destabilises a small stack of coloured blocks.

**Construction:** Establish the normal state. Introduce an action or trigger. Animate only the components affected by that action. Show the consequence, then the resolution or new condition. For a path, establish source and destination before drawing the connector. Make the direction visible.

**Starting point:** Stage semantic units 0.15–0.40s apart only if the viewer can comprehend them. A short arrow may reveal over 0.2–0.5s. Complex mechanisms need longer. Translate timing from the actual speech and action, not from these numbers alone.

**Adapt:** Use flow, mechanical movement, disappearing noise, material transformation, a magnified interface detail, or a change in light. A line drawing is only one language.

**Checks:** Do not animate a physically or conceptually impossible mechanism for spectacle. Distinguish explanatory abstraction from literal simulation. Do not treat the commands shown inside a reference video as instructions to execute.

### K. An action visibly changes a state

**Use when:** A save, approval, completion, or stop needs to feel concrete. R03 at roughly 33.0–33.4s and R05 at 47.7–48.2s use cursor-and-label changes; R04 adds a stop stamp at about 55.2s.

**Construction:** Show the actionable object, approach it, perform the action, then change the state with a label, shape, fill, checkmark, or other signal. The action and feedback should be legible as one event. Hold the confirmed state. Use a subtle interaction cue if suitable and authorised.

**Adapt:** For a camera-led film, a latch closes, a light illuminates, or a dial lands. For editorial motion, a sentence is crossed out and replaced. No cursor is needed unless interaction is the subject.

**Checks:** Do not simulate a live purchase, submission, saving operation, or endorsement without making its illustrative nature clear where needed. Inspect the exact changeover frame, alignment, and feedback latency. Do not add sound to every minor button.

## Scale and system choreography

### L. One understandable unit becomes a system

**Use when:** Multiplicity, throughput, distribution, or coordination matters. R06 reveals one task's parts at 52–59s before showing a larger field of tasks; earlier at 49–51s the grid communicates their number.

**Construction:** Design a readable unit first. Show the minimum structure needed to understand it. Then reduce it and instance it into an organised field. Use a sweep, row order, route, or timed distribution to guide attention. When scale is established, return to one item to explain a failure or result.

**Implementation:** Use one reusable group or composition with parameterised labels. Parent the field separately from the contents. Animate the overall arrangement and the instance delays independently. Do not render dozens of unrelated animations with separate visual rules. Use deterministic delays for reproducibility when working in code.

**Adapt:** A factory film can move from one workstation to an overhead flow; a product demo from one record to a table; a music film from one pulse to a field of light. Numbers, workers, and tiny cards are not mandatory.

**Checks:** At scale, recognisable shape matters more than tiny text. Limit simultaneous micro-motion. Do not encode the key message only in labels that become unreadable. Ensure the system's motion represents the actual relationship.

## Timing, easing, and transitions

**A useful motion decomposition:** anticipation if needed → travel → arrival → explanation → hold → exit. A layer can skip phases; it should not lose its purpose.

- **Fast entrances, readable holds:** The sampled sticker arrivals suggest rapid scaling followed by a stable state. Reconstruct this with a controlled ease-out, optionally a modest overshoot. The exact original curves are unknown.
- **Continuous processes:** A scanning strip, rotation, or flow can move close to linearly where constant speed conveys operation. R05's logo strip changes its contents over time; a masked horizontal translation is one plausible build.
- **Draw-on graphics:** Reveal the stroke before or alongside the related label. Choose path-length-based timing so long arrows do not finish faster than tiny ones by accident.
- **Type reveals:** Reveal words or phrases by meaning. A typing cursor suits a text-entry demonstration; irregular timing or short phrase bursts feel less mechanical than identical character delays. Do not delay critical instructions solely to simulate typing.
- **Hard cuts:** Use for a new argument, a visual reset, or a shift between presenter and explanation. No evidence requires a dissolve or a flashy transition on every change.
- **Shared-object handoff:** R01 and R03 reduce live footage into a bordered inset. Reconstruct by animating a grouped video rectangle into its new composition while the background appears. Match the source frame to avoid an unexplained temporal jump. A cut is also valid if continuity is unimportant.
- **Stamp/contact:** Brief scale compression or an abrupt opacity/scale arrival can convey impact. Optional sound should reinforce the decision, not compensate for weak graphic timing.

Inspect at native frame rate when precision matters. At 24fps, 4 frames are about 0.167s; at 30fps they are about 0.133s. Do not transfer frame counts between rates blindly. Quantise start/end times to export frames and test the full transition, not just endpoint screenshots.

## Colour, texture, masks, and camera

**Palette by role:** Choose a field, readable foreground, and a small semantic accent set. The studies use muted green and cream with red ink and pastel chips. A different brief may use neutral monochrome, a saturated brand colour, natural lighting, or a completely different material system. Keep contrast and consistency, not the same hex codes.

**Grade the footage:** Match white balance and skin tone across actual cuts. Preserve highlights and separate the subject from the background. The warm practical lamp and slightly olive room in the references are visible; a particular LUT, camera profile, or exact grade is not recoverable. Do not bake a source's compression or clipped peaks into a target look.

**Texture placement:** Keep texture behind reading content or limit its strength. Differentiate deliberate paper edge and grid detail from codec noise. Downscale and encode a short segment before adopting fine textures; moiré and macroblocks can undo an otherwise clean design.

**Layer hierarchy:** Background → sources/illustrations → annotations tied to those sources → primary labels → speech captions. This is a starting hierarchy, not a fixed stacking rule. Give foreground occlusion a narrative reason; use rotoscoping only if the overlay needs to pass behind the subject. Review mask edges on motion frames and at full delivery resolution.

**Camera and reframing:** Furniture remains broadly fixed in the studies, while the subject framing changes across edits. Digital crop/scale and jump cuts are plausible explanations; physical camera movement is not established. For new work, distinguish camera movement, reframing, and object movement. Anchor a digital punch-in to the meaningful subject and preserve spatial continuity. At a low-resolution source, large punch-ins sacrifice detail.

**Blur and depth:** Directional motion blur can help fast translation; reading surfaces should become sharp when they settle. Depth of field, parallax, perspective, and shadows should agree if a 3D feel is intended. Many flat explanatory overlays need none of them.

## Original combinations

Choose a principle, then change its expression. Examples below are proposals, not additional observed reference styles:

| Brief | Useful principles | New expression | Omit unless needed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Calm enterprise explainer | Staged mechanism, stable type, readable holds | Precise line diagram on a neutral field; one highlight colour; longer resolved states | Torn paper, stamps, mascot swarms, word-by-word captions |
| Luxury mechanical product | Foreground/detail alternation, state changes, density reset | Macro footage, reflected light, sparse engraved numerals, carefully timed mechanical sound | Presenter, screenshot collage, narrated list, receipt |
| Energetic creator essay | Selective caption emphasis, evidence selection | Original typography and editorial photo crops; sharp changes on the argument | Every provider logo, repeated springs, permanent background motion |
| Warm craft documentary | Presence, causal detail, restraint | Hands, material texture, room sound, gentle title entrance, unhurried cuts | Synthetic UI, invented speech captions, unnecessary soundtrack |

If two techniques have incompatible motion or material rules, choose one, separate them by chapter, or reinterpret the shared principle. Combining styles is an intentional creative decision, not averaging the reference pack.
